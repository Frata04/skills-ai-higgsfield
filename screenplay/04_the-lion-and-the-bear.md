## SCENE 6 — "THE LION"

---

**EXT. SHEEPFOLD — CONTINUOUS — NIGHT**

*Black. Sound only — the roar, sheep screaming, a struck lyre-note dying —*

**SMASH IN:**

The lion hits him like a collapsing wall.

There is no grace in it. David is simply *gone* — off his feet, driven
backward, the staff spinning out of his hand into the dark. He lands under
four hundred pounds of muscle, the air punched out of his lungs in a sound
that isn't human. The fire scatters — someone kicked it, or he did — and now
the whole world is strobing orange and black, dust already rising in a choking
wall as a hundred sheep detonate in every direction.

CLOSE — DAVID'S FACE. Pure animal terror. Not the noble calm of a moment ago.
His eyes are wide and white and rolling. This is a boy who is *dying* and knows
it.

The lion's jaws snap for his throat. David gets a forearm up — not bravery,
reflex — and the teeth close on his arm instead of his neck. The pain is
enormous, obscene. He SCREAMS. Blood, black in the firelight, sheets down to
his elbow.

> **DAVID**
> *(a raw, wordless howl — no words, just the sound of an animal in a trap)*

He is on his back, pinned, the great head thrashing to tear the arm it's got.
Its breath is a furnace. Saliva and his own blood spatter his face. One
back claw rakes his thigh open and he feels it go in, feels the strange cold of
a wound too deep to hurt yet.

He is not fighting it. He's just being *killed.* His free hand slaps the ground,
scrabbling, finding nothing — dirt, a bell, the hot edge of a scattered coal —

The coal. He grabs it. It sears his palm shut around it and he does not care.
He drives it into the lion's eye.

The animal recoils — a shriek, a jerk of the head — and the teeth come free of
his arm and for half a second David is *loose.*

He does not stand. There's no standing. He *rolls,* dragging his ruined arm,
scrambling on three limbs through the dust and the churning legs of panicked
sheep, and his good hand closes on wood — the staff.

He comes up onto his knees swinging it two-handed and connects with the side of
the lion's head with everything he has and everything he is. A dull, sickening
crack. The lion staggers. It is not down. It shakes its head, one eye streaming,
and turns back to him, and now it is not hunting. Now it is *furious.*

They face each other in the swirling dust. Boy on his knees, bleeding from the
arm and the leg, chest heaving, the broken half of the staff in his fist.
Lion, one-eyed, enraged, gathering. Firelight and dust and the screaming of
sheep.

And David — spent, shredded, seconds from death — does the only thing left in
him. He tips his face up and he SHOUTS it at the sky, not eloquent, not a psalm,
just torn out of him raw:

> **DAVID**
> *HELP ME —*

The lion springs the second time.

And this time David does not brace. He drops — flat, under the arc of it —
and jams the broken, jagged end of the staff *up,* bracing the butt against the
ground, and lets the animal's own weight and its own fury drive it down onto
the point.

The lion's roar chokes off into something wet.

Four hundred pounds crash down on top of him a second time and this time it
does not get up. It thrashes — an earthquake of muscle — David buried under it,
both hands white-knuckled on the shaft, holding it in, screaming through his
teeth, feeling the great body shudder and buck and slowly, slowly, over an
eternity of seconds — go still.

Silence.

Dust, drifting down through the last of the firelight. The far-off, ragged
bells of scattered sheep. And under the dead weight of the lion, a boy,
breathing in short, wrecked gasps.

**LATER — SAME PLACE — PRE-DAWN**

The fire is rebuilt, small. Grey light bleeds into the east. The lion lies where
it died, a dark hill. The sheep have crept back, drawn to the fire, milling
uneasily.

David sits apart from all of it, against the stone wall, and he is shaking.

Not a shiver. Shaking — his whole body, hands jumping, teeth chattering, the
uncontrollable full-body tremor of a person whose terror has nowhere left to go
now that the danger's gone. His mauled arm is bound in a strip of his own tunic,
soaked through dark. He can't make his hands be still. He watches them shake as
if they belong to someone else.

And then — quietly, without any drama — he begins to cry.

Not from pain. From the other thing. The tears just come, running through the
dirt and blood on his face, and he lets them, because there is no one here to
see, and because he is twenty years old and he just felt his own death breathing
on his neck and he is, somehow, impossibly, still here.

NADAV — who has been frozen against the far wall this whole time, too frightened
even to run — creeps closer. Stops a few feet away. Stares at David, at the
lion, at David again, with something past fear now. Awe. Terror. Love.

> **NADAV** *(a whisper, shaking)*
> You — you killed it. You killed a *lion.*

David doesn't look up right away. When he does, his face is wet and open and
he shakes his head — slow, certain, almost frightened of the truth he's
saying.

> **DAVID** *(barely a voice)*
> No.

He looks down at his own shaking, ruined hands. Turns them over.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)**
> I couldn't. I was — *(his voice breaks)* — I was already dead, Nadav. It had
> me. I felt it. There's no — a man doesn't — *(he can't finish it)*

He looks up, past the boy, at the greying sky. And the tears come harder, but
his voice, underneath them, goes very quiet and very sure.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)**
> Something held it off me. Long enough. *(a broken, wondering breath)* I called,
> and — something *held it off me.*

He presses the back of his good wrist to his mouth. Sits with it. The enormity.

Then, slowly, David gets up — grimacing, favoring the torn leg — and limps, not
toward the fire, but out among the sheep. He moves through them, counting the
only way he knows, until — at the edge, trembling, half-buried in the others —
he finds her. The little dark runt. Alive. Unhurt.

He lowers himself down beside her, wraps his good arm around her small body, and
presses his forehead into her wool. And he stays there, in the grey light,
holding the last one, shaking, while the sun comes up over the hills of Judah.

**SLOW FADE.**

## SCENE 7 — "THE BEAR"

---

**EXT. THE WADI — RAINY SEASON — DAY**

*A title, if we mark time at all: months later. But we don't need it — we can
see it. The hills are green now. David's forearm carries a long, healed,
puckered scar. He is not the same. He watches the tree-lines the way soldiers
watch doorways.*

A steep, rocky ravine slick with rain. Grey sky. The flock strung out along the
bottom, drinking from a swollen brown stream. David walks the high side, staff
in hand — a real one, iron-shod now — the sling already loose in his other
hand. He has learned. He does not wait for the eyes in the dark anymore. He
hunts the dark first.

Which is why, this time, he sees it before it sees the sheep.

Up the far slope, among the wet boulders — a BEAR. Bigger than the lion. Slower,
but heavier, a wall of dark sodden fur and slab muscle, nosing down toward the
water and the animals. It hasn't charged. It's deciding.

David does not scream. Does not freeze. His face goes hard and calm and
calculating — the exact opposite of the boy the lion found. He talks, low and
fast, to himself, working the problem:

> **DAVID** *(under his breath)*
> Not on the low ground. Not with the water behind me.

He's already moving — not away, but *sideways and up,* onto a shelf of rock,
putting height and footing under himself, getting the bear *below* him. As he
goes he loads the sling, spins it once, twice — testing the wind, the rain-heavy
air — and looses.

The stone cracks off the bear's skull. It rears with a bellow that shakes the
ravine — not hurt enough, just *enraged* — and it comes. Fast. Faster than
anything that size should move, an avalanche of muscle up the wet rock straight
at him.

This fight is uglier than the lion. Longer. There is no single desperate stroke.
It is *work* — brutal, grinding, exhausting work. David uses the height. Uses
the staff — jabbing, keeping the terrible reach of those claws at bay, giving
ground only when he must, never turning his back, never going down. The iron
shoe of the staff opens a gash over the bear's eye. A swipe of one paw shatters
the staff to splinters and nearly takes his head with it; he feels the wind of
it.

He is panting like a bellows. Soaked. Bleeding from a raked shoulder. His legs
are going. And the bear keeps *coming* — it will not stop, it does not know how
to stop —

David backs onto the narrowest point of the shelf, a lip of rock over a ten-foot
drop, and he understands, cold and clear, that he cannot outlast it and cannot
outrun it. So he stops running.

He waits. Draws it in. Lets it rear up full over him — that whole shrieking
mountain of teeth and claws blotting out the grey sky — and at the top of its
reach, when all its weight is up and back and its throat is open, David drives
the broken splintered shaft of the staff two-handed up under its jaw with a
guttural roar and throws his whole body behind it, over the edge —

— and lets its own falling weight tear it off the lip of the rock.

The bear goes over. David goes flat on the shelf, one hand clamped on a root, the
other bleeding, as the thing crashes down the ravine in a slide of rock and
water and finally, terribly, stops.

Silence, but for the rain and his own animal gasping.

David lies on his back on the wet stone for a long moment, chest heaving, rain
falling straight into his open eyes, too spent to even wipe it away. He does not
weep this time. He does not shake. He just breathes — ragged, enormous breaths —
and stares up into the grey pouring sky.

And when he finally speaks, it's not a scream and not a prayer of terror. It's
quiet. Almost conversational. Between him and the One he's decided, by now, is
always there.

> **DAVID** *(rasping, up at the rain)*
> ...The lion. And now this. *(a wet, exhausted, disbelieving laugh)* You
> keep sending them, and You keep — *(he shakes his head against the stone)*

He closes his eyes. Lets the rain come down.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)** *(barely audible, and utterly certain)*
> The same hand every time.

He lies there, torn and soaked and alive, on a rock in the rain, a young man
who has now twice met something that should have killed him and twice walked
away — and who is beginning, without any words for it yet, to believe he cannot
be killed until the promise is kept.

Below him, the flock drinks from the stream, oblivious, safe. Every one of them.
Even the last.

**HOLD. Rain. Then —**

**CUT TO BLACK.**

---

*END OF SCENE 7.*

*He does not know it yet — but the same certainty that just carried him off that
rock is about to walk him into a valley, in front of an army, toward a man
nine feet tall.*
