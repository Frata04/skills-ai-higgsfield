## SCENE 2 — "THE ONE WHO WAS MISSING"

---

**INT. JESSE'S HOUSE, BETHLEHEM — LATE AFTERNOON**

The largest room in a working man's house — which is to say, not large. Beaten
earth floor, swept clean for the occasion. A low table. The smell of roasted
meat and fresh-lit lamps. Through the doorway, the light is going amber.

The heifer has been offered. The blood is done. Now comes the part no one in
this room understands but the old prophet.

JESSE — sixties, weathered, a farmer and herdsman who has buried a wife and
raised eight sons and knows exactly what his family is worth in the eyes of
important men: not much. He stands too straight, the posture of a modest man
trying to be worthy of a guest too great for his house.

SAMUEL sits at the place of honor. Calm on the surface. But his eyes keep
moving — searching, waiting. He is listening for something the room cannot
hear.

> **JESSE** *(a nervous host, filling silence)*
> It's — it is not every year the seer of Israel sits at my table. My sons
> have talked of nothing else since midday. *(a small, anxious laugh)* I told
> them to wash.

Samuel gives him a thin, kind smile. Says nothing. The waiting is its own
pressure.

Jesse turns toward the inner doorway and lifts his chin — a signal.

> **JESSE (CONT'D)**
> Eliab. Come.

ELIAB enters. Firstborn. Tall — genuinely tall — broad through the shoulders,
a jaw like a cornerstone. A soldier's body and a soldier's certainty. He knows
how he looks. He crosses the room and stands before Samuel, and he does not
lower his eyes.

Samuel looks up at him. And something moves in the old man's face —
recognition, or the beginning of it. *Of course.* Height. Bearing. A head
taller than other men.

*The way Saul was.*

Samuel leans forward. His hand drifts, almost of its own will, toward the horn
at his side.

> **SAMUEL** *(low, certain, to himself)*
> Surely — surely this is the one. Standing here before Him.

And then — the room draws tight. The same tightening as the night in Ramah.
The lamps do not flicker. But Samuel goes still.

> **THE VOICE** *(quiet, private, meant for him alone)*
> Do not look on his appearance.

Samuel's hand stops.

> **THE VOICE (CONT'D)**
> Or on the height of his stature. I have rejected him.

*A beat. Samuel's certainty drains out of his face.*

> **THE VOICE (CONT'D)**
> Man looks on the outside. I look on the heart.

Eliab, seeing the old man's expression change — the reaching hand withdraw —
frowns, uncertain. Did he do something wrong? He glances at his father.

Samuel recovers. Sits back. Keeps his voice even.

> **SAMUEL**
> A fine son. *(a pause)* The LORD has not chosen this one.

The words land oddly on the room. Jesse doesn't understand what's being
weighed — but he hears *not chosen*, and a flicker of confusion crosses his
face. Chosen for *what?* He doesn't ask. You don't ask the seer.

> **JESSE** *(covering, gesturing Eliab back)*
> Of course. Of course. *(toward the door)* Abinadab.

ABINADAB enters — younger, leaner, quick-eyed. Presents himself. Samuel looks
at him and waits. Listens.

Nothing. The room stays slack. No tightening.

> **SAMUEL** *(gently)*
> Not this one either.

Jesse's brow furrows deeper now. He calls the next.

**SERIES — THE SONS PASS**

One after another they come. SHAMMAH, grave and careful. A fourth son, a
fifth, a sixth — each one presented, each one standing before the old prophet
under his patient, searching gaze. Each time Samuel waits for the air to
change.

Each time — nothing.

We see it wear on him. The confusion. The doubt creeping in. *Did I mishear?
Am I in the wrong house? Is God silent because I came in fear instead of
faith?* Between sons, Samuel's fingers press the bridge of his nose. An old man
questioning his own certainty.

The seventh son steps back into the line. Seven sons. All seven.

Silence.

Samuel looks at the row of tall, healthy, capable young men standing along the
wall — and none of them is the one. The waiting pressure has not come. He is
sure of only one thing now, and it is a strange one: *it is none of these.*

Jesse spreads his hands, at a loss. He has emptied his house. There is nothing
left to offer.

Samuel does not accept it. He leans forward. Quiet. Almost afraid of the
answer.

> **SAMUEL**
> Are these all the sons you have?

A small silence. And in it — Jesse hesitates. Just barely. The hesitation of a
man who did not think it worth mentioning. Who genuinely forgot to count one.

> **JESSE**
> There is — *(he almost waves it off)* — there is still the youngest.

*Samuel goes very still.*

> **JESSE (CONT'D)** *(almost apologetic, a shrug in the voice)*
> But he's out with the sheep.

The way he says it — *he's just a boy, he's out with the sheep, he's not the
kind of son you present to a prophet* — tells us everything about where this
last son sits in the family. An afterthought. Left in the fields on the one
day that mattered. Not even washed. Not even called.

Samuel rises to his feet. The whole room straightens in surprise at the old
man standing.

> **SAMUEL** *(with a quiet force that stops the room)*
> Send for him.

Jesse blinks.

> **SAMUEL (CONT'D)**
> We will not sit down until he comes here.

A beat of confusion — *for the boy?* — and then Jesse turns and snaps at a
servant, who bolts out the door toward the hills.

Samuel stands in the fading amber light, facing the empty doorway. And for the
first time since Ramah, the fear has left his face. What's there now is
something closer to wonder. He does not know the boy's name. He only knows the
room went quiet in the wrong place — and that God has been saving the last one.

**EXT. HILLSIDE ABOVE BETHLEHEM — GOLDEN HOUR — INTERCUT**

A flock scattered across a slope gone gold. And among them, a boy.

DAVID — sixteen, maybe seventeen. Not tall. Not broad. Ruddy-cheeked, sun-dark,
a shock of unruly hair. Dirt on his forearms, on his knees, under his nails. A
sling wound loose around one wrist like a habit he forgot he was wearing. He is
crouched beside a ewe, working a burr out of her wool with his thumbs, murmuring
to her the low nonsense a person says to an animal when they think no one is
listening.

He is not waiting for anything. He does not know his life is about to divide in
two. He is entirely, completely a shepherd, on an ordinary evening, doing an
ordinary thing.

The SERVANT crests the ridge at a dead run, chest heaving, and shouts his name
across the field.

David looks up. Squints. A burr still between his fingers.

**INT. JESSE'S HOUSE — MOMENTS LATER**

The door opens and David comes in — and the whole room turns to look at the boy
who was too unimportant to invite.

He is out of breath. He's aware, suddenly, of the dirt on him, of seven washed
brothers along the wall in their good cloth, of a great old man he's only heard
about standing in the middle of his father's house looking straight at him. He
stops just inside the door. Uncertain. A little embarrassed. Wipes a palm on
his tunic as if that would help.

> **DAVID** *(quiet, breathless, to the room)*
> I — I came as fast as I —

He doesn't finish. He doesn't know why he's here.

And then — the room changes.

Not for the boy. For the old man.

The air draws tight — that same silent tightening — and this time Samuel does
not have to search for it, does not have to lean in and listen. It *lands*. It
fills the room like a held breath. And under it, unmistakable, private, sure:

> **THE VOICE**
> Rise. Anoint him. This is the one.

Samuel's eyes fill. His mouth parts slightly. He is looking at a dirty
sixteen-year-old shepherd who is still catching his breath in a doorway — and
he is seeing a king.

No one else feels it. Jesse watches, baffled. The brothers exchange glances.
Eliab's jaw tightens — *the youngest? him?* — but no one speaks, because the
seer has begun to move.

Samuel crosses the room slowly. Deliberately. David's eyes widen as the old man
approaches, and he instinctively half-steps back, glancing at his father — *what
did I do, what is this* — but Jesse only stares, as lost as his son.

Samuel stops in front of the boy. Up close, he is even smaller than he looked.
The prophet studies his face — the sunburn, the smudge of dirt on one cheek, the
wide, honest, frightened eyes — and something breaks open in the old man's
expression. Tenderness. Grief. Awe. All at once.

He lifts the horn of oil.

David goes utterly still. He does not understand. But he understands *this* is
enormous, and he holds his breath.

Samuel tips the horn. The oil runs down over the boy's dark hair, over his
brow, catching the last gold light — slow, glistening, absurdly ordinary and
absolutely holy at the same time. It drips off the end of his nose. David
doesn't move. Doesn't wipe it. Just stares up at the old man, chest still,
eyes shining and afraid.

No one cheers. No one falls down. There is no proclamation, no crown, no crowd.
Only a farmer's crowded back room, seven silent brothers, an old man's trembling
hand, and a boy anointed in near-total quiet with sheep-dirt still on his knees.

Samuel's hand comes to rest on the crown of David's wet head. He holds it there.
And he says nothing at all — because there is nothing that would not be smaller
than the moment.

Then — David feels it.

We see it move through him. A breath he didn't take. His eyes lift, off Samuel,
past him, up — as if something vast has just walked into the room behind his own
ribs. His hands open at his sides. Something ancient and enormous has come to
rest on a boy who, an hour ago, was pulling a burr out of a sheep's wool.

He doesn't have a word for it. He will spend the rest of his life looking for
one.

The last of the sun goes out of the doorway.

In the near-dark, the old prophet and the shepherd boy stand facing each other,
oil shining, no one speaking — and outside, on the hills, the flock is bleating
for the boy who is not coming back tonight.

**SMASH CUT TO BLACK.**

*END OF SCENE 2.*
