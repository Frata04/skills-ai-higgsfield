# THE SHEPHERD KING
### A Historical Drama

---

## SCENE 1 — "THE HORN OF OIL"

---

**INT. SAMUEL'S HOUSE, RAMAH — NIGHT**

A low room of mud-brick and stone. One oil lamp, nearly spent, throws a
shallow ring of light. Beyond it, darkness. Rain ticks against the roof.

An old man sits on the floor. SAMUEL — seventies, white-bearded, a face
carved by decades of speaking words other men were afraid to carry. He is
not praying. He is staring at nothing.

In front of him, laid out on a cloth as if for burial, is a curved ram's
horn sealed with wax. A vessel of anointing oil. He does not touch it.

He has been weeping. The kind of weeping that has already stopped and left
the face wet and still.

He speaks. Quietly. To no one in the room.

> **SAMUEL**
> I put the crown on his head with my own hands.

Silence. The lamp gutters.

> **SAMUEL** *(barely audible)*
> I loved him.

He closes his eyes. A long breath leaves him.

Then — the room changes. Nothing moves. But the air draws tight, the way
air tightens before thunder. The lamp flame stands straight up and stops
flickering.

Samuel's eyes open. He does not look startled. He looks *tired*, like a man
who knows this voice and knows it never comes to comfort him.

> **THE VOICE** *(low, everywhere, felt more than heard)*
> How long will you grieve for Saul?

Samuel doesn't answer. His jaw tightens.

> **THE VOICE (CONT'D)**
> I have rejected him from being king over Israel.

> **SAMUEL** *(a whisper, almost an accusation)*
> I know what You have done.

He turns his face away — the exact human gesture of a man who does not want
to hear more, who wants to be left alone with his grief a little longer.

The flame holds. The Voice does not raise. It waits. Then:

> **THE VOICE**
> Fill your horn with oil. Go.

Samuel's eyes drift to the sealed horn on the cloth. His whole body resists
it.

> **THE VOICE (CONT'D)**
> I am sending you to Jesse, of Bethlehem. I have chosen a king from among
> his sons.

Samuel finally looks up — toward the ceiling, toward the dark, toward
wherever one looks. And now fear moves across his face. Real fear. The fear
of an old man who has watched a king lose his mind.

> **SAMUEL**
> How can I go?

His voice cracks. He steadies it.

> **SAMUEL (CONT'D)**
> If Saul hears of it —

He stops. Swallows. Says the thing plainly.

> **SAMUEL (CONT'D)**
> He will kill me.

The rain fills the silence. Samuel's hands, we see now, are trembling —
not with age.

The flame does not waver. When the Voice comes again, it is not angry. It
is almost gentle. The way you speak to someone you are asking to walk into
fire.

> **THE VOICE**
> Take a heifer with you.

Samuel listens.

> **THE VOICE (CONT'D)**
> Say: *I have come to sacrifice to the LORD.* Invite Jesse to the
> sacrifice. And I will show you what to do.

A beat.

> **THE VOICE (CONT'D)**
> You will anoint for Me the one I name to you.

The pressure in the room releases all at once — the flame flickers again,
normal, small. The rain sounds like rain again. The Voice is gone.

Samuel sits alone. For a long moment he does not move.

Then, slowly, he reaches out and picks up the horn of oil. He holds it in
both hands, in his lap, and looks at it the way a man looks at a thing that
is going to cost him everything.

> **SAMUEL** *(to the horn, to himself, wry and exhausted)*
> A heifer. So that when they ask, I have an honest answer to give them.

A short, broken breath that is almost a laugh.

> **SAMUEL (CONT'D)**
> Even You are teaching me how to lie.

He closes his fingers around the horn.

**CUT TO:**

---

**EXT. ROAD TO BETHLEHEM — DAY**

Wide. A single old man leading a young heifer by a rope along a stony ridge
road, the hills of Judah folding away behind him in the heat-haze. He is a
small figure in a vast, indifferent land. Dust rises from his sandals.

He does not look like a prophet on an errand from God. He looks like a man
who is afraid and going anyway.

**CUT TO:**

---

**EXT. BETHLEHEM — GATE — DAY**

A small hill town of terraced stone. Goats. The smell of dung-smoke and
threshed grain. Children stop their game to watch the stranger approach.

Word moves faster than Samuel walks. By the time he nears the gate, THE
ELDERS OF BETHLEHEM are hurrying out to meet him — six, seven men, robes
thrown on quickly, sandals half-strapped. Their leader, ADIEL — sixties,
broad, a man used to being the most important person in any room in this
town — reaches Samuel first and stops short.

Nobody bows. Nobody speaks. They just *look* at the seer standing at their
gate with a sacrificial heifer, and the blood drains from their faces.

A prophet does not walk to a town this size to bring good news.

Adiel's eyes flick to the heifer. To the horn at Samuel's belt. To the road
behind him, checking — instinctively — whether soldiers are following.

When Adiel finally speaks, his voice is not steady. He is a powerful man
trying very hard to sound calm.

> **ADIEL**
> Do you — *(he wets his lips)* — do you come in peace?

The other elders have gone dead still. One of the younger ones has taken a
half-step back. Fear, plain on every face. Whatever this old man says next,
their town will live or die by it.

Samuel looks at them. At their terror. And something in him — the part that
has spent a lifetime being the thing men are afraid of — is tired to the
bone of it.

He lets the silence stretch one beat too long. Cruel, almost. Then his face
softens, and he lifts one open hand.

> **SAMUEL**
> In peace.

The elders don't move. They don't believe it yet.

> **SAMUEL (CONT'D)** *(steadier now, the practiced calm of the lie)*
> I have come to sacrifice to the LORD.

He lets that land. Then, to Adiel, quietly, almost kindly:

> **SAMUEL (CONT'D)**
> Sanctify yourselves. Come with me to the sacrifice.

The relief that moves through the elders is physical — shoulders dropping,
one man exhaling a laugh he didn't know he was holding. Adiel closes his
eyes for a moment, a man who just watched a sword lowered from his own neck.

> **ADIEL** *(recovering, reaching for hospitality like a lifeline)*
> Of course. Of course. You honor us. We —

He turns to bark orders at the others — *water, the good oil, tell the
households* — the town already lurching back into motion around them.

But Samuel is no longer listening to Adiel.

Over the elder's shoulder, past the gate, into the little square — Samuel's
eyes have found something. A house. An ordinary house, low and pale in the
afternoon sun. He does not know yet which house it is. But he is looking for
it.

Because somewhere in this town of nobodies is the boy God has chosen. And
Samuel has come to put oil on his head and, in doing so, sign a warrant on
another man's throne.

He does not smile. He watches the town, and the fear in his own chest has
not left him. It has only changed shape.

Somewhere, a shepherd's boy is out on the hills with the flock, and no one
has thought to send for him.

**FADE OUT.**

*END OF SCENE 1.*
