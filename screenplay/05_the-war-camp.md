# ACT THREE

---

## SCENE 8 — "THE VALLEY OF ELAH"

---

**EXT. ISRAELITE CAMP, RIDGE ABOVE THE VALLEY OF ELAH — MORNING**

A war camp that has stopped being a war. Forty days of stalemate have rotted it.
Tents pitched too long, churned mud, cold fire-pits, flies. Men sit in the shade
doing nothing, because there is nothing to do but wait to be afraid again. The
smell of a place where thousands of men are quietly losing their nerve.

Across a wide valley — a dry streambed at the bottom, terebinth trees, pale
scree slopes — sits the other army. The PHILISTINES. Ordered. Bronze. Patient.
Their camp looks like a blade that has been waiting a long time to be used.

Onto the ridge road comes DAVID — dusty from the road, leading a laden donkey,
a cloth sack of provisions over his own shoulder. Bread, cheeses, parched grain.
An errand boy. His father's youngest, sent to check on his brothers and bring
the commander a gift. He is looking at a real army for the first time in his
life, and his eyes are wide — not with fear. With *wonder.*

He stops a passing quartermaster.

> **DAVID**
> The men of Judah — Jesse's sons — Eliab, Abinadab, Shammah — where do they —

> **QUARTERMASTER** *(not stopping, jerking a thumb)*
> Front line. Where else. *(a bitter grunt)* Best seats for the show.

David finds them at the forward edge of the camp — a knot of soldiers staring
down into the valley. ELIAB, older now, hard-faced, a real soldier gone gaunt
and mean with fear he won't name. He turns and sees his little brother standing
there with a sack of bread and a donkey, and something ugly moves across his
face — the specific fury of a frightened man who's just been handed something to
be angry about.

> **ELIAB**
> What are you doing here.

> **DAVID** *(warm, oblivious to the tone)*
> Father sent bread. And cheeses for your captain. He hasn't heard from you in —

> **ELIAB** *(cutting in, low and vicious)*
> Who's watching the sheep?

*A small silence. It's a slap, and it's meant as one.*

> **ELIAB (CONT'D)**
> Those few sheep. In the wilderness. *(stepping closer, voice dropping so the
> other men won't hear him savaging a child)* I know you, little brother. I know
> that heart of yours. You didn't come for Father. You came down here because
> you wanted to *watch.* You wanted to see the battle.

David holds his gaze. Doesn't flare. Doesn't cower. Just — absorbs it, the way
you absorb a blow you've taken many times before.

> **DAVID** *(quiet)*
> What did I do now? *(a beat)* Can't I even ask a question?

He turns away from his brother — not in anger, just done — and it's then, mid-
turn, that he hears it.

A HORN. Deep. Single. From across the valley.

And the entire Israelite front line — thousands of armed men — *flinches.*

**EXT. VALLEY FLOOR — CONTINUOUS — DAVID'S POV**

Down among the terebinths, the Philistine ranks part. And something comes
through them.

We hear him before we see him clearly. The tread. A slow, deliberate,
earth-taking tread, and the sound that comes with it — the massive articulated
*hiss and clash* of more bronze than one man should be able to carry, moving.

Then the trees give him up, and we see GOLIATH.

The scale is wrong. That is the first thing, and the worst thing — the human eye
simply refuses him. He is not a big man. He is a *different size of thing,* a
head and shoulders and half a chest above the largest soldier near him, and
those soldiers are his shield-bearers, and they look like children walking a
war-horse. Scaled bronze armor sheathes him like the hide of some deep-sea
animal. A spear whose shaft is the thickness of a weaver's beam. And above all
of it, a face — not stupid, not brutish. *Intelligent.* Bored. A craftsman
looking at raw material. He has done this every morning for forty days and he
has begun to enjoy the silence he causes.

He does not shout, at first. He just *stands* in the middle of the valley, and
lets ten thousand men look at him, and lets the looking do its work.

Along the Israelite line — CLOSE ON FACES. Veterans. Killers. Men with scars.
And every one of them finds something to look at that is not Goliath. The
ground. Their own hands. The sky. *Anywhere.* A soldier beside David crosses his
arms to hide that they're shaking. Nobody meets the giant's eyes. It is a whole
army performing the small, animal act of not being noticed by a predator.

Then Goliath speaks — and his voice carries the whole width of the valley
without effort, casual, almost warm, which is somehow worse than a roar:

> **GOLIATH** *(across the valley, unhurried)*
> Forty days. *(a pause, letting it land)* Forty mornings I have come down here,
> and asked one small thing. One man. *(spreading his arms, the spear catching
> the light)* Choose him. Send him down. If he kills me — we are your slaves. If
> I kill him — *(a slow smile)* — you are ours.

He turns slowly, sweeping the whole line, giving every man a moment to feel
personally addressed.

> **GOLIATH (CONT'D)**
> Is there not one? *(softer, mocking, intimate)* In all of Israel. One.

Silence. A crow calls somewhere. Ten thousand men look at their feet.

David is not looking at his feet.

David is standing very still, the sack of bread still on his shoulder, staring
down into the valley — not at the giant's size, but at the *silence* around him.
At the flinching men. At his own brothers, hard soldiers, eyes down. And on his
face is not fear.

It's *confusion.* Total, genuine, uncomprehending confusion. As if he has walked
into a room where everyone has agreed to believe something insane, and he is the
only one who can't.

He turns to the soldier beside him. Asks it simply, quietly, like a boy asking
why the sky is a color:

> **DAVID**
> Who is he? *(beat)* That he can say that. About the armies of the living God.

The soldier stares at him like he's mad.

David looks back down at Goliath. The giant turns, unhurried, and begins his
slow walk back to his own lines — the daily ritual, the daily nothing — trailing
that clash of bronze, leaving an entire nation ashamed behind him.

And David watches him go with the exact expression of a shepherd who has just
seen something moving toward the flock in the dark — and cannot understand why
no one else is standing up.

## SCENE 9 — "THE LION AND THE BEAR AND THE MAN"

---

**INT. SAUL'S COMMAND TENT — DAY**

Larger, darker, cooler. Maps weighted with daggers. The smell of oil and old
wine. This is where the war is not being fought.

SAUL sits at the center of it. And the years have not been kind. Still the
tallest man in the tent — the king was chosen for his height, once — but the
proud captain of Act One has sunk into something haunted. Heavy-lidded, gray at
the temple, a restlessness in his hands. A man being eaten from the inside by
the knowledge that God has left him and he cannot say so out loud. Around him,
his GENERALS — ABNER, the commander, broad and skeptical, chief among them.

A guard pushes David in. He looks absurd here — a sunburnt shepherd in a dusty
tunic, brought before the king because word got around that some *boy* was
asking loud questions on the line.

Saul looks up at him. Exhausted. Unimpressed. Waves the guard's explanation
away.

> **SAUL**
> They tell me you have something to say about the Philistine.

> **DAVID** *(steady, respectful, but plain)*
> Let no man's heart fail because of him. *(a beat)* Your servant will go. And
> fight him.

A silence.

And then ABNER laughs. A short, disbelieving bark. And it breaks the tension,
and one by one the generals join in — not cruel, exactly, but the helpless,
tired laughter of desperate men at the one funny thing that's happened in forty
days. Even Saul's mouth twists, the ghost of a bitter smile.

> **SAUL** *(not unkindly, which is worse)*
> You. *(he looks David up and down)* You are not able to go against this
> Philistine to fight him. You are a boy. *(gesturing vaguely at the valley)* And
> he — he has been a man of war since before you could walk.

The generals are still chuckling. David does not laugh with them. He does not
argue, either. He waits, patiently, for the laughter to run down — the way you
wait out weather — and when it has, he speaks. Not louder. Quieter. And he does
not defend himself. He just — tells them a true thing that happened to him.

> **DAVID**
> Your servant kept his father's sheep.

The last chuckle dies. Something in how he says it.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)**
> One night a lion came. Took a lamb out of the flock. *(he's not performing;
> he's remembering, and it costs him)* I went after it. I struck it, and it
> turned on me. *(he lifts his forearm — the scar, the long puckered scar, plain
> in the lamplight)* It had me on the ground. It had my arm in its mouth. I
> was — *(a breath)* — I was already dead.

No one is laughing now. Abner's arms have uncrossed.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)**
> I caught it by the beard, and I struck it, and I killed it. *(quietly)* And
> months after — a bear. Same thing. Out of the same flock. *(a small, tired
> shake of the head)* That one was worse.

He looks up. And here is the thing — there is no boast in his face. None. He
looks almost *puzzled* by his own survival, the way he did the morning after the
lion.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)**
> I want to be clear with you, my king. *(carefully, like it matters more than
> anything)* I did not save myself. A man doesn't — a man does not walk away
> from those. *(he taps the scar)* Something held them off me. Long enough. Every
> time I called.

Silence in the tent. Real silence now. The generals have gone still. Saul is
leaning forward, and the exhaustion has gone out of his eyes, replaced by
something he hasn't felt in a long time and can barely recognize on someone
else's face. *Faith.* Naked, unembarrassed faith.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)** *(gentle, certain, final)*
> The same hand that saved me from the lion, and from the bear, will save me from
> this Philistine. *(a beat)* He has defied the armies of the living God. *(the
> smallest shrug)* He is only bigger. He is not different.

For a long moment Saul just looks at this boy. At the scar. At the calm. At the
thing he himself lost somewhere on the road from Act One to here. His throat
works. When he finally speaks, his voice is rough — and there is grief buried in
it, the grief of a man watching someone else carry what was taken from him.

> **SAUL** *(quietly)*
> Go. *(a breath)* And the LORD be with you.

He says the last words carefully, like a phrase in a language he used to speak.

## SCENE 10 — "THE ARMOR"

---

**INT. SAUL'S TENT — CONTINUOUS**

Impulse takes Saul. He rises — suddenly urgent, almost desperate to *do*
something, to give this boy a chance, to buy back a little of his own soul by
arming him. He starts pulling his own royal armor from its stand.

> **SAUL** *(fast, insistent)*
> At least — here. Take mine. Take my armor. *(to a servant)* The bronze. The
> helmet — quickly —

And now a strange, quiet, almost unbearable little scene. They dress David in
the king's armor.

It is enormous on him. Saul is a head taller than any man in Israel; David is
not tall. The bronze coat hangs off his shoulders like a tent. The helmet slides
down over his brow. They cinch straps that have no more holes to cinch. A
servant kneels, frowning, trying to make it *work,* and it will not work, and
everyone in the tent can see it will not work, and no one says so.

David stands there, swallowed in the armor of the king. He takes a step. The
weight nearly buckles him. He takes another, the sword now belted on, and he
sways — a boy playing at being a soldier, drowning in metal made for a bigger,
prouder, hollower man.

Everyone watches. Abner. The generals. Saul, with a terrible hope on his face —
*please let this work, please let me have helped.*

David stops trying to walk. He stands still in the great hanging bronze, and he
looks down at himself. And there is no contempt in his face, and no drama. Only
a plain, kind honesty as he reaches up and begins, carefully, to unbuckle the
helmet.

> **DAVID** *(quietly)*
> I can't go in these.

He lifts the helmet off. Sets it down, gently, so as not to insult it.

> **DAVID (CONT'D)** *(as he unstraps the coat)*
> I've never worn them. *(a small, apologetic almost-smile)* I don't know how to
> move in another man's armor.

The coat comes off. He hands it back — the king's bronze, the king's protection,
the king's whole idea of how a man survives — folds it into a servant's arms
with something close to tenderness.

And then he is just David again. A shepherd in a dusty tunic, standing in the
middle of a command tent full of armored men. Smaller than everyone. Utterly
unprotected. And, somehow, the calmest man in the room.

He picks up his staff from where he leaned it by the door.

Nobody speaks. Abner watches him with something that is no longer scorn and not
yet respect — something more like dread. Saul stares at the folded armor in the
servant's arms, then at the small figure by the door, and his face is doing
something complicated and wounded, as if he is watching a truth he spent his
whole reign refusing.

David pauses at the tent flap. Looks back at the king. And he says it not as a
boast, but almost gently — the way you'd reassure someone who's more frightened
than you are:

> **DAVID**
> It was never the armor that saved anyone.

He steps out into the light.

The tent flap falls shut. Inside, silence. Saul sinks slowly back into his seat,
staring at nothing, one hand rising to his own mouth.

**CUT TO:**

*The white light of the valley. And a boy, walking down toward the streambed,
picking up stones.*
