# ACT FOUR — "THE BROOK"

*A held breath. The film slows down and refuses to hurry. No score. No cutting
for tension. Long takes. The camera is patient, because David is.*

---

## SCENE 11 — "FIVE SMOOTH STONES"

---

**EXT. VALLEY FLOOR, THE STREAMBED — DAY**

We leave the armies behind. The sound of the camp — ten thousand men, the
horns, the fear — thins and falls away behind the ridge until it is gone, and
what replaces it is almost shocking in its ordinariness:

Wind. Just wind, moving through the terebinth trees, turning the pale undersides
of the leaves. A bird somewhere, unbothered, singing the same three notes it
would sing on any morning. And under it all, low and constant — water. A thin
brown thread of a stream, winding through smooth stones at the bottom of the
world's most famous valley, doing what it has done for ten thousand years,
indifferent to everyone above it.

Into the wide shot walks DAVID. Small. Alone. A single figure crossing an
enormous emptiness, his staff in one hand, the sling coiled at his belt. He is
not marching. He is not steeling himself. He walks the way a shepherd walks
across a familiar hillside — unhurried, at home in open ground.

We stay wide a long time. Let him be small. Let the valley be large. Let the
wind and the water do the work that music would do in a lesser film.

He reaches the brook and stops.

For a moment he simply stands over the running water, looking down at it. The
light comes off the surface in moving coins. He watches it move. His face is
unreadable — not afraid, not brave, just *present,* the way a man is present
when he has decided to stop carrying the future for a moment and only be where
his feet are.

Then, slowly, David kneels.

Not to drink. Not yet to gather. He kneels at the edge of the water the way a
man kneels at a threshold, and he lowers his head, and he closes his eyes, and
he is still.

We hold on him. The wind moves his hair. The bird sings its three notes. The
water runs. He does not perform the prayer. His lips barely move. Whatever
passes between him and God here is private, and the camera does not intrude on
it — it simply waits, at a respectful distance, and lets us watch a young man be
quiet in the presence of the thing he trusts.

A long beat. Longer than is comfortable. Long enough that we hear the individual
sounds — a fly, the creak of the trees, his own slow breath.

When he opens his eyes, nothing has changed in the world and everything has
settled in him.

He reaches into the stream.

**CLOSE — THE WATER, HIS HANDS**

Cold, clear-brown water breaking over his fingers. He runs his hand along the
bed of the brook, through the gravel and the rounded stones, and he begins to
choose.

He is not grabbing at random. He is a craftsman. He lifts a stone, turns it in
his fingers, feels its weight, its balance, the smoothness of it against his
thumb — the same hands that mended a lyre string, that whittled a pin, that
worked a burr out of a ewe's wool. He knows exactly what a stone must be to fly
true from a sling. Too light, it drifts. Too rough, it drags. Not round enough,
it will not sit in the pouch.

He finds one that is right. Water-smoothed, dense, the size of a hen's egg. He
weighs it. Nods, almost imperceptibly, to no one. Sets it in his palm.

He finds a second. Turns it. Discards it back into the water with a small plip —
not good enough. Chooses another in its place.

A third.

A fourth.

He does this without urgency, without ceremony, the small ordinary competence of
a man who has done a physical thing ten thousand times and does it well. The
water runs over his wrists. The bird sings. Somewhere, over the ridge, an army
waits and a giant waits, and none of that exists here, at the brook, where a
shepherd is simply choosing good stones from a stream because good stones are
what the work requires.

A fifth.

He holds all five in his cupped palm. Looks at them. Five smooth stones, wet,
catching the light.

**WIDE — DAVID AT THE BROOK**

He tips them, one by one, into the shepherd's bag at his side. We hear each one
settle — five small, distinct sounds.

Then David rises.

He stands over the running water, and he takes the sling from his belt, and he
lets it hang loose and ready from his hand — a strip of worn leather, cracked
and darkened with years of use, the most ordinary object in the world. He looks
at it a moment. This little thing. Against that.

He does not smile. He does not frown. He looks up, over the water, toward the far
slope where the ground rises back up toward the enemy line — and the peace on
his face does not break. It hardens, very slightly, into resolve.

The wind gusts. The trees turn silver. The bird goes quiet.

And David — alone, small, unarmored, five stones and a strip of leather — turns
away from the brook and begins to walk toward the rising ground.

We do not follow him yet. We stay at the water. We let him walk out of the frame
and leave us there, at the brook, with the wind and the running stream — and
then, at the very edge of the shot, far up the slope, we hear it begin again:

The horns. The distant murmur of two armies. The world he is walking back into.

The stream runs on, and does not care.

**SLOW FADE.**

*END OF SCENE 11.*

*The next time we see him, the valley will be looking back.*
